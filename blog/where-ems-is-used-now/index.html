Where EMS is showing up now: space, hospitals, sport and VR

Electrical muscle stimulation is not new. What is new is where it keeps appearing: space agencies, intensive care units, sports labs and virtual reality. Here is what the research of 2024–2026 actually says.

The idea is simple. Small electrical impulses reach a muscle through pads on the skin, and the muscle contracts. Doctors call it NMES (neuromuscular electrical stimulation). In fitness it is usually WB-EMS: whole-body EMS, a suit with pads on the main muscle groups, worn while you move.

Over the last two years EMS has turned up in some unexpected places. We went through recent studies and official reports and kept only what has a solid source.

Space: keeping muscles when gravity is gone

Without gravity, leg muscles have little to work against and start to shrink. In July 2025 the European Space Agency wrote that it “is testing neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES)”, which “applies gentle electrical pulses to the leg muscles”.1 The study uses MRI scans, microcirculation analysis and blood samples.

On Earth, scientists simulate weightlessness with long head-down bed rest. A NASA-funded study at the German Aerospace Center’s :envihab facility keeps volunteers in bed for 60 days and tests daily stimulation of the leg muscles, 30 minutes per session. The first campaign ran from September to November 2024.2 Results have not been published yet.

Hospitals: when patients cannot move

The same logic applies to people stuck in bed after illness. A 2024 meta-analysis pooled 23 randomized trials with 1,798 critically ill patients. Electrical stimulation improved a standard muscle-strength score, but it did not change death rates or time on a ventilator.3

A smaller 2024 study put 16 young and 16 older adults on five days of bed rest. One thigh got NMES three times a day for 30 minutes. That thigh kept its muscle thickness, though not its maximal strength.4

Researchers are also comparing options for older adults with sarcopenia, the age-related loss of muscle. A 2025 network meta-analysis of 22 trials and 1,062 patients ranked electrical stimulation with protein well for fat reduction. Classic resistance training still came out best for muscle.5

Sport: promising, but honest

Here the picture is mixed, and we prefer to say so. A 2025 evidence map counted 39 WB-EMS studies in people who exercise. Professional athletes were rarely studied.9

  • Power: a 2026 meta-analysis of 13 trials found a positive effect on maximum lower-body power. The endurance results were inconclusive.6
  • Jumping, sprinting, agility: another 2026 meta-analysis of 12 studies found only “limited additional effects” of WB-EMS on these skills.7
  • Warm-up: in a small 2025 study with 10 semi-professional football players, adding WB-EMS to the FIFA 11+ warm-up did not significantly improve sprint times.10

EMS is a tool, not magic. It works best as part of real training, with a coach who knows the method.

New technology: feeling the virtual world

EMS is also becoming an interface. At the CHI 2025 conference, researchers from MIT and partner institutions presented TelePulse. The system takes forces measured by a remote robot and “converts these torques into muscle stimulation”, so a person in VR can feel what the robot feels.8

Fitness: why the method matters

In WB-EMS the stimulation reaches many muscles at once, so dose matters. The international safety guideline, updated in 2023, says beginners should do no more than one 20-minute session per week for the first 8–10 weeks. It also says: “Non-supervised WB-EMS application must be strictly avoided.”11

A 2024 study shows why. After a single high-intensity 20-minute session, a blood marker of muscle damage peaked at 72 hours, and people responded very differently.12 The same session can be easy for one person and too much for another.

The bottom line

EMS has moved far beyond the gym. Space agencies, intensive care teams and VR labs all use it for the same reason: it makes muscles work when normal training is hard or impossible. In sport and fitness the evidence is growing but mixed, which is why a careful, supervised method matters more than the suit itself.
